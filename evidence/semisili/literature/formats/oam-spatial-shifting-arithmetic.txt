Introduction to Optical Digital Computing
Architecture
The historical trajectory of computing architectures has been predominantly defined by the
evolution of electronic semiconductor devices, governed for decades by the empirical
predictions of Moore’s Law and Dennard scaling. However, as transistor gate lengths approach
fundamental thermodynamic and quantum mechanical limits—most notably governed by
Landauer's principle regarding the minimum energy required to erase a bit of information—the
constraints of the von Neumann bottleneck have become critically pronounced. The energy
dissipation and inherent latency associated with shuttling electrons between physically
separated memory and processing units necessitate the exploration of alternative,
non-electronic computational substrates. Optical computing has emerged as a premier
candidate to transcend these limitations, leveraging the ultra-high bandwidth, extreme
parallelism, inherent multiplexing capabilities, and near-zero energy consumption of passive
photon propagation1.
While analog optical computing has seen significant advances in specialized tasks such as
Fourier transforms, spatial filtering, and matrix-vector multiplications, the realization of
general-purpose optical computers remains strictly contingent upon the development of
robust, scalable all-optical digital logic and arithmetic architectures. The primary challenge lies
in executing precise mathematical operations without relying on power-hungry
optical-to-electrical-to-optical (O-E-O) conversions or highly sensitive optical non-linearities,
which require immense power densities to trigger1.
Recent advancements in nanophotonics and structured light have identified the orbital angular
momentum (OAM) of light as a pivotal degree of freedom for encoding multidimensional
information. Unlike intrinsic spin angular momentum (SAM), which is physically constrained to
two orthogonal states corresponding to left- and right-circular polarization, OAM possesses a
theoretically infinite state space2. By structuring the phase front of a laser beam into a helical
shape, photons can be imbued with a quantized topological charge. Concurrently, the advent
of Optical Diffractive Neural Networks (ODNNs) has provided a highly flexible framework for
manipulating complex optical fields. Composed of cascaded layers of passive phase and
amplitude modulators, ODNNs can be trained in silico using deep learning algorithms and
deployed as physical optical hardware to perform specific tasks at the speed of light4.
A groundbreaking synthesis of these domains is the utilization of ODNNs to perform fixed-base
digital arithmetic through the spatial shifting of OAM modes. Rather than relying on continuous
phase alterations to directly scale the topological charge of a single beam, this paradigm
exploits the spatial positioning of independent OAM beams to represent the magnitude of a
digit in a positional numeral system. This maps arithmetic multiplication and division directly
onto the physical left and right spatial shifting of these optical modes across a transverse

diffractive plane4. The subsequent analysis provides an exhaustive examination of this
architecture, detailing the theoretical quantum physics of OAM, the diffraction mechanics of
ODNNs, the mathematical mapping of fixed-base arithmetic onto spatial coordinates,
alternative geometric coordinate transformations, and the operational robustness of these
systems under physical and atmospheric perturbations.

In Silico Training and Phase Quantization
The optimization of the ODNN phase matrices relies on simulating the physical diffraction
process in silico and utilizing standard deep learning backpropagation algorithms. The training

=== PAGE 5 ===
loop consists of information forward propagation, loss calculation against an ideal target
output, error backpropagation through the diffractive layers, and subsequent weight (phase)
updates5. Algorithms such as Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) are employed to minimize
loss functions (e.g., Mean Squared Error or categorical cross-entropy) within computational
environments equipped with high-performance graphics processing units5.
Upon the convergence of the loss function, the theoretical continuous phase profiles must be
discretized for physical fabrication or dynamic display on spatial light modulators (SLMs). Phase
quantization introduces an element of physical error into the ideal mathematical model.
Analyses confirm that while binary phase modulation introduces significant modal degradation,
phase quantization at or above 4-bit levels (16 discrete phase steps) provides sufficient
degrees of freedom to accurately execute OAM spatial shifting operations without
catastrophic fidelity loss4. This relatively low-bit requirement is highly advantageous for
physical implementations.

Encoding Arithmetic in Orbital Angular Momentum Arrays
To implement this mathematical isomorphism optically, the discrete data bits must be reliably
encoded into physical optical signals. OAM modes serve as the optimal information carriers
due to their mutual orthogonality and highly distinct spatial intensity profiles. In the
ODNN-based arithmetic unit, a logical "1" at a specific bit position is physically represented by
the presence of an OAM beam generated at a precisely defined spatial coordinate on the
transverse input plane. A logical "0" is represented by the absence of optical power at that
coordinate.
If an optical input array consisting of spatially distributed OAM modes—often referred to as an
OAM Shift Keying (OAM-SK) signal—is injected into the ODNN, the network's optimized phase
screens are tasked with routing each incoming OAM beam to its adjacent spatial coordinate to
the left4. By flawlessly executing this spatial shift without altering the topological charge, the
ODNN optically computes a left-shift logic operation, outputting an OAM array that represents
the multiplicand effectively multiplied by the operational base.
The profound advantage of this method lies in its massive physical parallelism. Regardless of

=== PAGE 7 ===
the bit-width or the complexity of the input integer, the spatial shift occurs simultaneously for
all encoded bits as the light wavefront propagates through the physical diffractive layers4.
Unlike sequential electronic Arithmetic Logic Units (ALUs) that suffer from carry propagation
delays, the latency of the optical arithmetic unit is bounded entirely by the time of flight of
photons traversing the physical distance of the ODNN—typically taking only fractions of a
nanosecond.
Rotational Reconfiguration and Multi-ary Logic Expansion
A remarkable feature of the phase screens optimized for this architecture is their capacity for
multi-functional reconfiguration through simple geometric manipulation. The operation type
can be switched dynamically from multiplication to division without requiring a completely
separate ODNN hardware module. By mechanically or electro-optically rotating the

intermediate phase matrix by               clockwise, the diffractive properties of the screens are
altered such that the resulting optical transfer function mirrors a rightward spatial shift4.
Consequently, the input OAM array shifts to the right, enabling a computational architecture
that seamlessly supports both fundamental functions using identical hardware4.
Furthermore, the architecture is not restricted to binary logic systems. By expanding the
semantic mapping of the spatial modes and utilizing multiple amplitude or phase levels,

higher-radix computing can be achieved. In a 2-bit ternary configuration (base                              ), the

positional weights represent powers of 3 (                        ). When the ODNN executes a

decimal value by 34. The successful manipulation of ternary and quaternary data further
highlights the unparalleled scalability of optical spatial shifting arithmetic compared to
binary-locked electronic architectures.

Error Tolerance
The transition of optical computing designs from idealized numerical simulations to physical
hardware is often bottlenecked by extreme sensitivity to manufacturing tolerances, mechanical
misalignment, and environmental phase noise. Evaluating the ODNN-based arithmetic unit
involves rigorously scrutinizing its mode purity, diffraction efficiency, and robustness to
physical and atmospheric perturbations.

Robustness to Mechanical and Physical Misalignments
A physical optical neural network is susceptible to various spatial perturbations during
assembly and operation. Three principal sources of hardware error must be analytically
addressed: lateral displacement, rotation deviations, and phase quantization.
Lateral displacement arises from imperfect optomechanical alignment between the incident
laser source, the input modulation plane, and the cascaded ODNN layers. The ODNN arithmetic
unit exhibits a high degree of shift-invariance within tight spatial bounds. Minor lateral
displacements on the sub-millimeter scale degrade mode purity slightly but generally maintain
a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio for the OAM topological charge to be reliably decoded at the
output plane4.
Rotation deviations pose a more critical threat. Because the toggling of the arithmetic unit

between multiplication and division relies on an exact                   clockwise rotation of the phase
matrix4, angular deviations during mechanical or electro-optic rotation can severely corrupt the
spatial routing path. The performance degradation curves indicate that while small angular

misalignments of less than            are well-tolerated, larger deviations lead to a rapid breakdown
of the spatial shifting matrix, dispersing the OAM beam across the output plane as
unstructured speckle noise4.

Integration into All-Optical Logic and Networking
Ecosystems
The utility of a fixed-base spatial shifting arithmetic unit is maximized when it is contextualized
within a comprehensive, integrated all-optical processing ecosystem. Arithmetic manipulation
is only one fraction of a generalized Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU); logical gating and signal
multiplexing are equally vital.
All-Optical Logical Gates and Adders
To complement physical multiplication and division, ODNNs have been successfully trained to
execute fundamental logical boolean operations directly on OAM modes. Empirical research
demonstrates that basic logic gates—including AND, OR, NOT, NAND, and NOR—can be
mapped directly into single-model ODNN architectures10.
In the implementation of an OAM logic AND gate, the presence of specific topological charges
serves as the binary inputs. The diffractive network evaluates the physical presence of these
incoming fields and outputs an OAM beam with a specific target topological charge if, and only
if, both input modes are active10. The performance of these gates is exceptionally high; for
instance, the ODNN accurately completes the logic OR operation with an output mode purity
reaching 99.13%10.
However, non-linear logic functions such as XOR and XNOR present a profoundly more
complex computational challenge. The data distribution features in the training data for XOR
are fundamentally "fuzzier" and lack simple linear separability. Consequently, a single passive
linear ODNN layer cannot easily resolve XOR operations while maintaining high mode purity. To
bypass this limitation, researchers physically cascade basic AND, OR, and NOT gates to
construct XOR and XNOR networks, successfully achieving the requisite operational truth
tables10.
By unifying these cascaded logic gates, ODNN-based OAM Half-Adders have been
successfully constructed. When the four fundamental input states ("00", "01", "10", "11") are
sequentially fed into the diffractive half-adder, the system yields output mode purities greater
than 91% for the SUM and CARRY operations10.

Optical Add-Drop Multiplexers (OADM) and Advanced Encryption
Moving vast quantities of computational data into and out of the arithmetic unit requires
sophisticated, low-latency routing. ODNNs serve dual purposes as high-efficiency Optical
Add-Drop Multiplexers (OADMs), capable of selectively coupling and separating distinct OAM
modes for signal transport5.
In a simulated four-channel communication link operating at 1550 nm, these OADMs
demonstrate the ability to process multi-channel OAM signals carrying dense 16-Quadrature
Amplitude Modulation (16-QAM) data payloads. The diffractive networks achieve a remarkably
low Optical Signal-to-Noise Ratio (OSNR) penalty of approximately 1 dB at a stringent Bit Error
Rate (BER) of                    5. The integration of OADMs with spatial-shifting arithmetic units
implies that a central optical processor can not only compute complex mathematical
operations but also dynamically route the output integers into specific network channels based
exclusively on their assigned topological charge. This facilitates seamless all-optical
interconnections across data centers without the prohibitive latency of intermediate O-E-O
conversions5.
Furthermore, the infinite dimensionality of the OAM state space enables advanced
hardware-level optical encryption. By leveraging the specific diffractive routing patterns of the
arithmetic unit alongside spatial phase mapping, OAM mode logic can encrypt transmitted
imagery and data. An encrypted image cannot be obtained or resolved correctly if incorrect
OAM modes or mismatched multiplication/division transformations are applied during the
physical decoding phase at the receiver plane, thereby guaranteeing the physical-layer security
of information transmission during FSO communications3.
Broader Implications and Non-Linear Expansions
While the fixed-base spatial shifting mechanics rely heavily on linear diffractive optics,
theoretical expansions suggest integrating these paradigms with non-linear quantum
substrates. Advanced optical traps formed by intersecting LG beams can confine Bose-Einstein
Condensates (BECs) in highly structured optical potentials14.
In these environments, the potential depth and harmonic confinement are dictated by the

=== PAGE 14 ===
optical power and waist of the intersecting OAM beams. The dynamics of the condensate are
rigorously governed by the non-linear Gross-Pitaevskii Equation (GPE), which models the
kinetic, potential, and non-linear interactions of the trapped matter-waves14. By integrating
ODNN spatial shifting concepts with BEC optical trapping, researchers could theoretically
execute spatial bit-shifts that rely not just on passive diffraction, but on the deterministic
non-linear collision of quantum states, paving the way for ultra-low energy, quantum-coherent
arithmetic logic units.
Conclusion
The application of Optical Diffractive Neural Networks for fixed-base digital arithmetic
represents a watershed evolution in the architecture of all-optical computing. By
mathematically redefining the fundamental operations of digital multiplication and division as
uniform left and right geometric shifts of a positional bit sequence, and subsequently mapping
these bitwise operations to the precise physical spatial shifting of discrete Orbital Angular
Momentum arrays, researchers have successfully bypassed the fundamental latency and
energy bottlenecks of electronic circuitry.
The primary findings of this paradigm underscore its immense viability. The computational
elegance of executing left-shift multiplication and right-shift division operations using the

identical three-layer ODNN hardware—toggled purely through a                           clockwise physical
rotation of the phase matrix—highlights a remarkable efficiency in component utilization.
Operating at the industry-standard telecom wavelength of 1550 nm, the spatial shifting
mechanism proves inherently dimension-agnostic, supporting not just binary arithmetic but
extending naturally to ternary, quaternary, and broader high-radix computations without
requiring substantial hardware overhauls.
Driven by the rigorous mathematical orthogonality of OAM topological charges, the diffractive
process preserves mode purities up to an extraordinary 99.65%, maintaining the stringent error
tolerances critical for reliable data processing. The demonstrated empirical robustness against
lateral displacements, minor angular deviations, and extensive phase quantization further
confirms its readiness for physical, solid-state implementation. In synergy with all-optical
logical gates, OAM half-adders, and dynamic Optical Add-Drop Multiplexers, the fixed-base
spatial shifting arithmetic unit provides the definitive quantitative core required to construct
fully integrated, Turing-complete optical processors. As deep-learning methodologies for
optimizing these diffractive matrices continue to advance, this architecture is uniquely
positioned to redefine the speed, efficiency, and fundamental physical constraints of
next-generation high-dimensional computing networks.
