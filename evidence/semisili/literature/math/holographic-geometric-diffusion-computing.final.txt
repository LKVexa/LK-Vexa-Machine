Processable Holographic Computation:
Simulating Geometric Diffusion through
MHz-Rate Optical Field RAM-Sampling
and Dynamic Pixel Relocation
The paradigm of optical computing has historically been constrained by a persistent dichotomy
between passive data storage—such as high-density holographic memory—and active, yet
structurally rigid, matrix-vector multiplication architectures. A groundbreaking evolution in this
domain is the emergence of the "processable hologram." This framework redefines the optical
field not merely as a repository of static volumetric data, but as an active, massively parallel,
and continuously evolving computational medium. This architectural leap represents a
fundamental departure from classical von Neumann computational constraints, instead utilizing
the physical properties of wave optics to compute solutions through the physical simulation of
continuous geometric diffusion.
The fundamental ethos of the processable hologram relies on abandoning the sequential,
pipeline-driven processing characteristic of traditional digital logic. Instead, the system is
designed to project all computational possibilities and all information-bearing pixels
simultaneously. By representing the entire computational field in a single coherent state, the
system evaluates all localized interactions in parallel through free-space diffraction and optical
interference. Operating at an optimal Megahertz (MHz) refresh rate, this optical state space
evolves rapidly. The computational loop is actively closed by RAM-sampling the resulting
optical field and feeding the digitized optical state into a low-latency C# software harness.
During this RAM-sampling phase, the software harness analyzes the optical readout, identifies
specific information-bearing pixels, and dynamically relocates them across the spatial light
modulator (SLM) array. This relocation ensures that the pixels project their phase and amplitude
information from a different physical origin point, effectively mapping them to a different
location within the simulated numerical space or topological shape that the hologram
represents. The subsequent re-projection and re-sampling of these altered fields allow the
system to simulate highly complex phenomena. Principally, this iterative relocation and
projection cycle represents computation via geometric diffusion, where the relocation of
information naturally follows the gradients of differential equations operating on arbitrary,
evolving manifolds.
This report provides an exhaustive, multi-disciplinary analysis of the theoretical physics,
mathematical models, optoelectronic hardware, and software engineering mechanisms that
govern the five-stage processable holographic compute cycle.
1. The Ethos of Parallel State Representation
The initial stage of the processable holographic cycle demands the simultaneous
representation of the complete computational field. In classical digital systems, variables are

processed through discrete logic gates in a serialized pipeline, meaning that large state spaces
must be explored iteratively or through highly energy-intensive cluster parallelism. In contrast,
the processable hologram projects the complete field—comprising both the established data
points (information-bearing pixels) and the untraversed solution space (encoded
possibilities)—as a single complex amplitude distribution.
1.1 Volumetric Encoding and the Superposition of Possibilities
In volume holography, information is exclusively encoded within the spatial structure of optical
fields
1
. While traditional volume holography focuses on ultra-high-density archival
storage—approaching theoretical limits of petabits per cubic centimeter by multiplexing
volume holograms with two-photon spectral hole-burning in specialized crystals
1—the
processable hologram utilizes this massive density for active, simultaneous computation.
When a coherent light source illuminates a highly multiplexed Spatial Light Modulator (SLM), the
resulting optical wavefront acts as a physical superposition of all possible computational states.
Every pixel acts as an independent neuron or computational node, transmitting
continuous-variable information via its phase and amplitude. As the light propagates through
free space, these nodes interact with one another at the speed of light. The interference
pattern generated at the focal plane or sampling plane represents a global evaluation of all
localized interactions simultaneously.
By representing the entire field together, the system projects every "possibility" as a distinct
spatial frequency or phase modulation. The computation relies on how these possibilities
constructively or destructively interfere, effectively executing a physical Fourier transform or a
diffractive neural network forward pass in zero elapsed time, limited exclusively by the
picosecond-scale time-of-flight of the photons
2
. The encoding of these possibilities relies on
the precise calibration of the SLM, ensuring that the phase retardation introduced at each pixel
accurately maps to the desired initial conditions of the simulated numerical space.
1.2 Hardware Constraints: The Necessity of MHz-Rate Modulation
To evolve this field dynamically, the system requires a mechanism to alter the spatial
distribution of light at rates that can outpace or match high-speed electronic feedback loops.
Conventional liquid-crystal-on-silicon (LCoS) SLMs suffer from intrinsically slow modulation
mechanisms. Due to the physical relaxation times of nematic liquid crystal molecules and the
limitations of electrical addressing circuits, traditional LCoS devices are typically capped at
refresh rates between 60 Hz and a few kilohertz
3
. While Digital Micromirror Devices (DMDs)
based on microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) offer faster mechanical actuation in the
tens of kilohertz, they are generally limited to binary amplitude or binary phase modulation,
which is insufficient for the continuous-variable mathematics required in geometric diffusion
3
.
To achieve the optimal MHz operating rate required for continuous geometric diffusion
simulation, the processable hologram must utilize advanced electro-optic materials governed
by the Pockels effect. Materials such as barium titanate ( or BTO) exhibit massive
electro-optic coefficients (e.g., ) and feature ultrafast response times in
the sub-picosecond range
6
.

index shift detunes the resonant frequency of the microcavity, yielding a complete to

Because the Pockels effect relies on the displacement of electron clouds rather than the

6

hologram, operating at an "optimal MHz rate" is a critical architectural choice. While the optical
hardware can push into the multi-GHz regime, the MHz rate represents the optimal
harmonization point between the physical evolution of the SLM and the electronic
RAM-sampling latency required for the C# software harness to perform logical pixel
relocations without dropping frames or inducing system desynchronization.

2. Evolving the Field via Free-Space Optical Operators
Once the complete field is represented on the MHz-capable SLM, the second phase of the
compute cycle involves the evolution of that field. This evolution is not computed
algorithmically by a processor; rather, it occurs physically as the optical wavefront propagates
through free space. The physics of optical diffraction act as the core computational engine,
processing the highly parallel encoded possibilities.

3. RAM-Sampling the Resident Field
The processable hologram is fundamentally distinct from a static optical projector because its
output is continuously monitored, digitized, and reintegrated into the compute cycle. This third
step is achieved through high-speed RAM-sampling, wherein the optically evolved field is
captured, digitized, and held in an electronic memory buffer to aid the performance of
subsequent computations.
3.1 Photonic Detection and Conversion Constraints
To read information and identify which pixels require relocation, the continuous-wave optical
field must be discretized by high-speed photodetector arrays coupled with Analog-to-Digital
Converters (ADCs). In high-bandwidth photonic computing, the readout latency represents the
primary bottleneck. The lowest achievable electronic latency is realized via Flash ADCs, which
feature an estimated delay of approximately 80 picoseconds for their dynamic comparators

--- PAGE 6 ---
and 30 picoseconds for their encoding gates
13
.
This physical limitation bounds strictly latency-dependent photonic feedback networks to
operating speeds of approximately 10 Giga-Samples per second (GS/s)
13
. However, because
the processable hologram operates optimally in the MHz regime to sync with the software
harness, the ADCs are operating well within their maximum theoretical bandwidth. High-speed
photodetectors capable of 32 Gb/s and ADCs capable of 32 GSPS are commercially mature
technologies, ensuring that the inversion rate and sampling rate of the optical field are not
limiting factors
14
.
To sample at the target MHz regime effectively without dropping data, the optical field is
focused onto the sensor arrays. Experimental photonic RAM architectures, which leverage
phase-change materials (PCM) like GST alloys or ultra-low power nanocavities, allow for
integrated optical buffers where the read speed is dictated merely by the photon's
time-of-flight
2
. Nevertheless, to interact with the computational logic of the C# harness, this
photonic data must cross into the electronic domain.
3.2 Bypassing CPU Overhead with PCIe and DMA
The RAM-sampling process converts the optical interference pattern—which represents the
physically solved state of the current geometrical diffusion step—into an electronic numerical
matrix. For the system to maintain its MHz operating rate, it cannot rely on traditional
CPU-mediated data transfers. Standard interrupt-driven I/O protocols introduce substantial
latency and jitter, which would instantly desynchronize the SLM projection from the software
state.
Instead, the hardware utilizes Direct Memory Access (DMA) over a high-bandwidth Peripheral
Component Interconnect Express (PCIe) bus. A PCIe frame grabber or custom optical readout
board equipped with a DMA controller handles the data transfer from the ADC directly into the
system's physical RAM16
. The DMA controller bypasses the Central Processing Unit entirely,
executing zero-copy memory writes into pre-allocated memory buffers
18
.
This architecture guarantees that the resident field is sampled and digitized with deterministic,
microsecond-level latency. The resulting data structure in the RAM provides the C# software
harness with a real-time, high-fidelity snapshot of the encoded possibilities and their
interference patterns, ready for algorithmic evaluation.
4. Pixel Relocation via the C# Harness
With the optical field successfully RAM-sampled, the computation transitions from the
continuous physical domain into the discrete software domain. The core objective of the C#
harness in step four is to read the sampled information, evaluate it against the target
computational state, and determine which specific pixels require relocation. The harness then
alters the SLM coordinates of these pixels so they project their information from a different
spatial location in the subsequent cycle.
4.1 Unmanaged Memory and Real-Time C# Constraints
C# is traditionally a managed, object-oriented language utilizing an automatic Garbage
Collector (GC) to handle memory allocation and deallocation

. In high-performance, real-time
control systems—such as an optical feedback loop operating at MHz rates—standard C#

causing the SLM to idle or project stale frames.
To circumvent the limitations of the managed environment, the software harness relies heavily
on the unsafe code context within C# and utilizes Platform Invocation Services (P/Invoke).
When the DMA controller writes the optical matrix into physical RAM, it deposits the data into
pinned, unmanaged memory buffers

utilize raw C-style pointers to directly access these memory addresses

This approach completely bypasses the .NET Garbage Collector, allowing the harness to iterate
over the massive pixel arrays in real-time. Modern SDKs designed for spatial light modulators,
such as those integrated via the Bonsai API or specialized PCIe controllers, natively support
these low-level memory operations, achieving processing times of less than 10 milliseconds
even for complex per-pixel exposure control
23
.
4.2 The Logic of Spatial Relocation
The act of identifying which pixels to move around is the logical crucible of the processable
hologram. In this paradigm, the pixels are not static image elements; they are active vectors of
information carrying amplitude, phase, and physical coordinates.
When the C# harness reads the unmanaged DMA buffer, it evaluates the local intensities and
phase gradients of the sampled optical field. An algorithmic filter—such as a gradient descent
solver or a topological clustering algorithm—identifies pixels that represent converged
computational solutions, or conversely, those trapped in sub-optimal local minima.
Once identified, the C# harness calculates new coordinates for these pixels based on the
topological rules of whatever space or shape the hologram is simulating numerically. By issuing
commands to the SLM SDK, the harness physically alters the origin point of that specific plane
wave on the BTO microcavity array.
This relocation fundamentally alters how that pixel's information will diffract and interfere in the
next cycle. By moving a pixel to a new location on the SLM, its angular spectrum contribution is
shifted
9
. If the hologram is simulating a specific numerical shape—such as a fluid dynamic
containment field, an encrypted manifold, or a high-dimensional neural network parameter
space—relocating the pixels effectively molds the boundaries and interior metrics of that
shape
9
.
A vital third-order insight reveals that this C# relocation acts as a mechanism for non-local
topological interaction. While physical wave propagation is strictly local and bound by
continuous free-space metrics, the software harness can instantaneously "teleport" a pixel
from one side of the simulated numerical space to the other. This allows the computational
system to bridge disjointed manifolds, escape thermodynamic equilibrium states rapidly, and
inject macroscopic boundary changes that would be impossible in strictly continuous physical
systems.

6. Systemic Implications and Broader Applications
The integration of the five-step processable hologram cycle establishes a computational
architecture that transcends traditional bounds. By framing computation as a geometric
diffusion problem solved via the physical manipulation of light, several groundbreaking
applications and systemic implications emerge.
6.1 Optoelectronic Neuromorphic Accelerators
Current optical computing systems, such as diffractive optical neural networks, generally
feature fixed diffractive layers or rely on slow LCoS updates, rendering time-multiplexed
recurrent architectures difficult to scale and adapt in real-time
5
. By incorporating MHz-rate
BTO SLMs and the C# DMA feedback loop, the processable hologram functions as an
ultra-high-speed optoelectronic recurrent neural network. The pixel relocations can be used to
dynamically re-route optical weights and biases. When a pixel is relocated, its diffraction
pattern interacts with different neighboring pixels, effectively re-wiring the neural network's
topology on the fly based on the sampled gradient of the loss landscape.
6.2 Bypassing the Von Neumann Bottleneck
In conventional high-performance computing, the Von Neumann bottleneck restricts
theoretical performance limits due to the limited data throughput between the CPU and the
system RAM. The processor spends excessive clock cycles waiting for data fetches. The
processable hologram bypasses this by utilizing the free-space optical cavity as both the
processor and the volatile memory. The massive parallel calculation (the diffusion) occurs
photonically. The electronic DMA-to-C# harness is only invoked for topological updates (pixel
relocation). This drastically reduces electronic power consumption while providing processing
speeds capped only by the temporal resolution of the optical-to-electronic conversion
components
5
.
6.3 Advanced Surface Processing and Encryption
Because the system inherently calculates the Laplace-Beltrami operator, it is highly optimized
for surface fairing, noise removal, and texture processing on 3D meshes
26
. Anisotropic
geometric diffusion, which modifies the diffusion coefficient at geometric edges to preserve
sharp features while smoothing planar regions, can be simulated effortlessly by programming
the C# harness to restrict pixel relocation near high-intensity gradients
26
.
Furthermore, random phase encoding in volume holographic storage is a unique technique for
image encryption
36
. The dynamic relocation of pixels by the C# harness can serve as a
continuously evolving cryptographic key. Because the interference pattern relies on the exact
sub-wavelength position of millions of pixels, intercepting the RAM-sampled readout is useless
without the real-time C# relocation algorithm that defines the topological geometry of the
next cycle.
7. Conclusion
The processable hologram represents a sophisticated, multi-disciplinary synthesis of wave

optics, advanced materials science, high-speed electronic engineering, and differential
geometry. By rejecting the static nature of classical holography, this paradigm embraces the

Through the continuous projection of all encoded possibilities and information-bearing pixels,
the system harnesses the deterministic, speed-of-light propagation of waves—governed by

The integration of MHz-rate electro-optic SLMs, driven by the Pockels effect in barium titanate

Crucially, it is the integration of the C# software harness via low-latency RAM-sampling and
PCIe DMA that elevates this system from a simple optical calculator to a dynamic,

information-bearing pixels using unsafe unmanaged memory environments, the software
actively molds the simulated numerical space. This relocation mimics the evolution of manifolds
via Mean Curvature Flow and the Laplace-Beltrami operator, allowing the system to solve
highly complex Partial Differential Equations on changing domains effortlessly, completely
avoiding geometry-physics confounding.
In essence, the ethos of the processable hologram is the absolute unification of physical optics
and digital topology. It utilizes the C# software harness to dynamically shape the geometry,
and relies on the physical diffraction of light to compute the diffusion. This iterative, non-local
feedback loop opens a promising and highly scalable frontier for optoelectronic computing,
establishing a robust foundation for the next generation of predictive PDE modeling,
neuromorphic processing, and complex topological simulation.
